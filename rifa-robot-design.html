RIFA World Cup · U13 · Robot design brief

Build a shuttle, not a striker

The scoring half is a 30 cm round trip you can run twenty times in ninety seconds — but you only ever get ten balls. The match is decided in the preparation half.

30 × 30 × 30 cm at each start expand to 35 cm in play ≤ 6 motors 90 s + 2 min + 90 s controller allowed (U13)

Read the rules first · G5 · G6 · G14 · G15

Where the match is actually won

Your ball dispenser sits on the end of the central divider, roughly 30 cm from the penalty-area line you must not cross. Press button, catch ball, drive 30 cm, kick, reverse 30 cm, press again. A clean robot runs that loop in four or five seconds — call it fifteen to twenty cycles in the ninety-second scoring half.

You will never get fifteen shots, because G14 caps the dispenser at ten balls and G15 caps you at ten goals. You start the scoring half with six standard balls. The only way to reach ten is to carry in four bonus balls from the preparation half.

Your score ceiling is set 90 seconds before you take a single shot

6 Goals possible if you collect no bonus balls
10 Goals possible with all four bonus balls collected
4 White cones = a near-solid wall across a 29.5 cm goal mouth G10.5
~4 s Target shuttle cycle — dispenser to shot and back

So: build the fast, boring shuttle second. Build the collector first, and spend your practice hours on the preparation half. A team that collects 4 balls + 4 cones beats a team with a beautiful kicker and an empty dispenser.

Geometry · Appendix A · §4.3

The lane that removes aiming from the job

The goal is 29.5 cm wide and centred on the table's long axis — so roughly 15 cm of the goal mouth lies on your own side of the divider. If your robot runs a lane about 8–12 cm off the divider, a shot fired dead straight ahead goes in. No turning, no aiming, no driver skill spent on alignment. Build a low skid or idler wheel on the divider side so the robot physically self-aligns against the 4 cm divider and drives the lane on rails.

Base · §3.1 · §3.2

Chassis: small, low, and two motors only

Your half of the table is a 57 cm wide corridor. A robot built out to the full 30 cm limit leaves 27 cm of clearance and turns like a bus. Build at roughly 20 × 22 cm and you gain far more from agility than you lose in sweep width — the sweep width comes from folding arms instead.

Drive on two encoder motors plus a caster. Mecanum or 4WD would eat four of your six motors §3.2 and leave nothing for mechanisms. With open budget, spend it on a second controller, a good kicker servo and spare drivetrains — not on more wheels.

Mechanisms · §3.4 · G10.3

Two attachments, swapped in the two-minute break

The halves ask for opposite machines: one herds fragile upright cones gently, the other fires a ball across 30 cm. Don't compromise into one head. §3.4 explicitly lets you change attachments, and G10.3 gives you a two-minute break to do it. Build both, make them swap on two thumbscrews, and get both inspected — §3.5.5 only permits parts that already passed inspection.

Attachment A

The Corral

Preparation half · 90 s · collect balls, then cones
  • Folding arms. Hinged at the front corners, folded to 28 cm for the sizing box, servo-spread to 34 cm once the whistle goes. Legal expansion under §3.1.2 and it nearly doubles your sweep.
  • Nothing grabs anything. Read what the rules actually ask: push balls into a 30 × 30 area and leave them G6.2, push cones into a 30 × 7 strip and leave them G7.1. Both are push-and-release. A passive plow beats any roller or gripper here and cannot jam.
  • Blade height is the one number that matters: 25–30 mm, with a 3 mm ground gap. That is above a 40 mm ball's centre, so balls get pushed down and forward instead of rolling over the top — and below the ring on top of the cone, so you only ever push the cone's base and it slides upright. Build the blade taller and you tip cones, and a tipped cone is worthless G7.4.
  • One servo, three arm positions. Folded at 28 cm for the sizing box · gathering at 34 cm in a shallow inward V · released, swung wide so you can reverse away and leave everything behind. Without that third position you drag your own cones back out of the strip.
  • The table wall is your backstop. Side walls are 7–9 cm A.1. Drive cones into the wall, open the arms, reverse. No mechanism needed to stop them.
  • Soft inner faces. Line the arms with foam or rubber strip so balls damp instead of bouncing back out of the mouth.
  • Keep Home clear. Any ball or cone that enters Home is dead for the half G8. Plan routes that never sweep through your own start box.
  • Black cone marker. Two of the six cones are black; touching or tipping one cancels a white G7.3 / P03. In U13 your driver's eyes are the sensor — give them a clear sight line over the mouth and a low front profile.
Attachment B

The Striker

Scoring half · 90 s · press, catch, kick, repeat
  • Passive button plunger. A sprung nub on the front face at the dispenser button's height — you press it by driving into the dispenser. No motor, no aiming step. Mount it on a slotted beam so you can set the height at the venue.
  • Single-ball throat. Make the catch pocket physically unable to hold two balls. P06 voids the extra goal if you carry more than one, and a second ball out of the dispenser is a referee interruption you don't want.
  • Single flywheel and hood, not a flipper. One ungeared 775 brushed DC motor (12 V, ~18,000 RPM no-load) spinning a 70–80 mm grippy wheel against a curved 2 mm polycarbonate hood. A geared Makeblock motor at ~180 RPM will never do this. Start compression at ball diameter − 4 mm; too much squeeze stalls the wheel instead of launching.
  • The hood angle is on a servo — this is how you beat the goalkeepers. Hood down fires a flat bowling shot; hood up chips the ball over the defensive line. Two shot modes off one actuator.
  • Own power. A 775 stalls near 20 A and will destroy an mBot motor port. Drive it through a BTS7960 H-bridge or a 60 A brushed RC ESC, on a separate 3S LiPo (11.1 V, 1500–2200 mAh, 30C), grounds tied to the controller.
  • Single-ball throat. Make the catch pocket physically unable to hold two balls. P06 voids the extra goal if you carry more than one, and a second ball out of the dispenser is a referee interruption you don't want.
  • Spin up early, never at the line. The flywheel needs 0.5–1 s to reach speed. Run it from the moment you leave the dispenser; a servo gate decides when the ball enters the nip.
  • Put the speed on dial-able presets, not full throttle. You get at most ten balls all half. A shot that is too hot and misses costs a whole goal off your ceiling G15.
  • Never shoot from Home. A goal doesn't count if any part of the robot is inside Home at last contact G15.

Press → catch → shoot · G14 · P06

The robot never turns around

The dispenser sits on the divider and the goal is past it in the other direction. Put the button-presser and the shooter both on the front and you spin 180° every cycle — roughly 0.7 s each way, and it is exactly where your alignment drifts over ninety seconds.

So put the presser on the rear. Reverse into the dispenser, the ball enters at the back, travels forward through the robot, and fires out the front. The robot faces the goal for the whole scoring half. That is what makes a four-second cycle real, and it is what the divider guide rail exists to support.

Stage 1 · rear

Press

Passive — and it doubles as your docking sensor
  • Height-adjustable finger. A rubber-tipped rod on a carriage sliding in a slotted beam, so you set it at the venue once you have measured the button. Nobody knows this height yet — the rulebook never states it.
  • Sprung, with a hard stop. ~10 mm of travel on a light compression spring, so the spring sets the press force rather than your driver's throttle. You cannot damage the field element §9.3.
  • One microswitch does two jobs. When the finger bottoms out, the switch closes — that single signal says both "the button registered" and "you are docked". Reverse until it closes, dwell 0.3 s, go. No distance sensor needed.
  • Guide horns. A shallow V flanking the finger cams you into alignment as you reverse. With the horns and the dispenser as a hard stop, reversing in is more repeatable than driving forward at it.
Stage 2 · middle

Catch

Passive — the ball is its own gate
  • Funnel mouth. Lip 3 mm off the mat, ~70 mm wide narrowing to ~45 mm, tall enough to catch a ball that rolls out or drops out — you do not yet know which the dispenser does.
  • A channel exactly one ball long. Shape the entrance lip so a seated ball physically blocks the next one. That satisfies P06 mechanically instead of asking an eleven-year-old to remember it at match pace.
  • Tilt it 5° forward on a slick floor — Delrin, PTFE or smooth ABS — so the ball always rolls to the same seat against a front stop ridge a centimetre short of the nip. Two ridges, one job each: the entrance ridge is the one-ball gate, the front ridge is what the paddle pops the ball over.
  • One IR reflectance sensor (TCRT5000 class) in the channel floor drives a "ball loaded" light. You never want to arrive at the line empty.
Stage 3 · front

Shoot

The only actuators in the whole chain
  • Flywheel spins continuously through the half. The servo paddle decides when — 90° throw, snapping back immediately so the next ball can seat.
  • The failure that will bite you: the front stop ridge must positively hold the ball clear of the spinning wheel. If a seating ball so much as brushes it, the shot fires instantly and at random — and you have lost one of your ten balls, probably over the divider.
  • Keep the paddle stroke short. Seat the ball about 1 cm short of the ridge so the servo only has to shove it ~3 cm into the nip — a 4 cm arm through 60°. A long stroke is what makes servo feeders unreliable.
  • The front line sensor stops you on the penalty-area line G16. Between it and the rear microswitch, the entire shuttle is closed-loop on two cheap sensors.

Beating the defensive line · G10.5 · G16 · G21

Their wall only covers the bottom third of the goal

The opponent places up to four white cones touching their goal line as goalkeepers G10.5. Four cone bases across a 29.5 cm mouth looks like a sealed goal, and teams lose matches trying to thread a 4 cm ball through the gaps.

But the goal is 25 cm tall and the cones are roughly 8–10 cm tall. Their wall blocks the bottom third. There is about 15 cm of open goal above every goalkeeper they place, and you are shooting from 30 cm away. The answer to the defensive line is not more power — it is an arc.

25 cmGoal mouth height
~10 cmCone height — measure this yourself, it is not in the rulebook
~15 cmOpen goal above their defensive line
2.2 m/sLaunch speed at 35–40° to clear a 10 cm cone at 30 cm range

That 2.2 m/s figure is the useful surprise: the chip shot is slower than the flat bullet, not faster. You are not fighting their wall, you are going over it, and the vertical window between the cone tops and the crossbar is wide and forgiving at this range. Set the hood, find one PWM number on the practice table, and the defensive line stops mattering.

The second tactic, and its caveat

Your robot may never touch a cone inside a penalty area — that is a red card G16. But nothing in the rulebook stops your ball from knocking their cones over, and G21 specifies that penalty kicks are taken "without goalkeepers", which treats the cones as plain obstacles rather than protected elements. So one hard flat shot as a demolition ball, then an open goal for the rest of the half.

Confirm this with the referees before you build a strategy on it. It is an absence of a rule, not a stated permission, and §9.6 leaves unaddressed cases to the Head of Refereeing Committee.

Card avoidance · G9 · G11 · G16 · G17

The cheapest points on the table are the ones you don't give away

A red card parks you in Home for 30 seconds — a third of the half — and a yellow costs 10. At a four-second cycle, one red card is worth more than seven shots. In U13 you're allowed sensors and code even with a driver on the sticks, so put the rules into the hardware rather than trusting a nervous eleven-year-old at match pace.

Red card · 30 s dead

Penalty-area cut-out

A downward line sensor at the extreme front edge. When it reads the penalty-area line, cut forward drive and flash the CyberPi red. Covers entering either penalty area G16 / P04, which is the single most likely card you'll take.

Red card · 30 s dead

Divider guide

The idler that rails your scoring lane also stops you climbing the 4 cm divider. Entering the opponent's half or touching their elements is a red card G11, and so is contact that obstructs their robot G17.

Yellow card · 10 s dead

Hands-off discipline

Touching the robot with any part of it outside Home is a yellow G9.2 / P01. Drill one rule with the team: fully inside Home, then hands. Mark Home's footprint on your practice table in tape.

Yellow card · 10 s dead

Dead sticks until the whistle

Any movement before the referee's whistle is a false start; the second one in a half is a yellow G2.5 / P10. Hold the gamepad with both thumbs off the sticks, and disable drive in code until a start button is pressed.

Treated as part of your robot

Nothing falls off

A detached part left on the field stays there and counts as your robot for the rest of the match P07. Threadlock every screw, tape over the battery latch, and shake-test the robot hard before each match.

Order violation

Ball before cone

Any white cone collected before your first bonus ball is fully inside the Ball Collection is void G5 / P02. This is free to get right and expensive to forget — it is the first line of your driver's script.

Match plan · G1–G19

The playbook your driver memorises

Preparation half — 90 s

0:00Whistle. Deploy arms to 34 cm and drive to the nearest bonus ball. Do not touch a cone yet.
0:00–0:25Sweep bonus balls into the 30 × 30 Ball Collection. Ball one is the priority — it unlocks cone scoring G5. Then finish all four: each one raises your goal ceiling by one.
0:25–1:15White cones into the 30 × 7 strip, one or two at a time, arms open to release, reverse clean. Partly inside counts G7.1; knocked over does not G7.4.
1:15–1:30Mop up whatever is closest. Drive the last ten seconds away from black cones — a late tip costs you a white cone you already earned.
breakRobot to Home. Swap to the Striker, fit a fresh battery, set the plunger height. Place your white cones touching the goal line, upright, spread across the mouth G10.5.

Scoring half — 90 s

0:00Whistle. Straight out of Home into the lane — remember a goal is void if any part of you is in Home at last ball contact G15.
the loopPress → catch → run the lane → stop on the line → kick → reverse. The divider guide does the steering; the line sensor does the stopping. Your driver's only real job is throttle.
on a missRecover your own ball only if it's still in your half and out of the penalty area. If it's gone, it's a Lost Ball — go back to the dispenser immediately, don't chase it.
if hitIf an opponent's illegal contact stops you, get the referee's eye. G17 lets you restart from Home with no penalty and no wait.
1:30Final whistle. A ball crossing the line after time does not count G19 — take the last shot by about 1:25.

Technical inspection · §3.5

Pass inspection, or forfeit 3–0

§3.5.4 is blunt: if your match is called before your robot has passed, you lose it 3–0. Build a 30 cm cube gauge out of cardboard on day one and put the robot through it at the end of every build session.

  • Fits 30 × 30 × 30 cm with each attachment fitted — checked before both halves, not once§3.1.1
  • Fully deployed, no dimension exceeds 35 cm — measure the arms at full spread§3.1.2
  • Motor count is 6 or fewer — count servos too, and confirm with the referees that they count§3.2.2
  • No liquids, no adhesives, nothing that can mark or damage the mat or another robot§3.2
  • Both attachments presented and approved at inspection, plus any spare part you might fit mid-match§3.5.5
  • No sharp edges, no exposed battery terminals, nothing that can injure a referee reaching onto the table§9.3
  • Spares in the pit: charged battery, drive motor, kicker servo, a full set of screws§3.4

Before you cut metal

Seven numbers the rulebook doesn't give you

Appendix A specifies the table, goal, home and collection areas — and then stops. Every one of these missing figures changes a dimension on your robot. Email the organising committee, and measure them yourself at the first practice session you get.

  1. Penalty-area dimensions. Not stated anywhere. My scaled estimate is ≈30 cm deep × ≈52 cm wide — it sets your entire shot distance.
  2. Ball diameter and mass. Sets your throat width, your intake roller gap and your kicker geometry. The photos look like a ~40 mm hollow ball.
  3. Cone height, base diameter and mass. Sets the blade height that slides a cone instead of tipping it.
  4. Dispenser button height above the mat, and the force to press it. Sets your plunger height and how hard you may drive into it.
  5. Dispenser ball-exit height and direction. Sets where your catch pocket sits and which way you approach.
  6. Whether servos count toward the six-motor limit. Get this in writing before you design a six-actuator robot.
  7. Whether the goal mouth straddles the centreline as Figure 4.1 shows. The whole "shoot straight down the lane" strategy rests on it.
